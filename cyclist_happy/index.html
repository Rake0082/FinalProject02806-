Behind every number is a person. Let's look at what the data reveals about who is most at risk — and why.

The Bigger Picture

A key distinction runs through every part of this analysis: the difference between how often something happens and how deadly it is when it does. The two do not necessarily move together, and this gap is precisely where prevention policy must be aimed. Reducing total crash volume calls for targeting high-frequency, everyday factors; eliminating fatalities requires targeting high-lethality ones.

Across the data, the most common causes of crashes are rarely the deadliest: driver inattention dominates in volume, while speed and alcohol are rarer but far more likely to turn a crash fatal. Vehicle type shapes survival odds consistently, and the busiest intersections are not the most dangerous ones. The data suggests that vulnerability in traffic is not primarily a matter of individual behavior — it is shaped by structural factors that require structural responses.

The analysis also points toward how much more remains to be explored — including a deeper look at infrastructure and a more thorough examination of whether initiatives like Vision Zero have delivered measurable change. As the data makes clear, there is still work to be done. These are not accidents. They are outcomes — and these outcomes can and must be changed.

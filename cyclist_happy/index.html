Behind every number is a person. Let's look at what the data reveals about who is most at risk — and why.

Summary

Cyclist collisions in New York City follow clear patterns in both time and space. Crash volume peaks during the afternoon commute and rises sharply in summer — but both reflect increased exposure rather than increased danger per trip. Where collisions happen matters as much as when: some of the city's quietest intersections carry the highest fatality rates, while the busiest ones often do not. The data shows that survival in a crash is shaped by factors ranging from age and ejection status to the type of vehicle involved — with box trucks posing by far the greatest fatal risk. Underlying all of this is a consistent finding: the most common causes of cyclist crashes are not the most deadly ones, and addressing both requires different strategies. Reducing total collisions calls for better infrastructure and driver awareness; eliminating fatalities calls for targeting speed and impairment.

Summary

Pedestrian collisions in New York City are shaped by a complex interplay of time, place, demographics, and behavior. Crash frequency peaks during rush hour, reflecting the density of shared urban space rather than an inherently more dangerous environment. While women and men are involved in collisions at nearly equal rates, men face a significantly higher fatality rate — a disparity that persists across the data. Geographically, the busiest boroughs generate the most incidents, but the deadliest clusters are often found elsewhere. Age is the strongest predictor of a fatal outcome, followed by the hour of the day and what the pedestrian was doing at the moment of impact — a factor that raises broader questions about whether infrastructure is designed to match how people actually move through the city. The vehicle involved also matters significantly: being struck by a box truck is more than five times as likely to be fatal as being struck by a standard passenger car.

Summary

Motorist collisions follow the rhythm of urban traffic — peaking during rush hours not because driving is inherently more dangerous then, but because more vehicles share the road simultaneously. When traffic volume is accounted for, the afternoon hours still carry a disproportionate share of crashes, suggesting that congestion itself may elevate risk. Weather adds another layer: on days identified by road salt usage as snow or ice days, the average daily collision count rises by roughly 26%. Geographically, the highest crash volumes concentrate in Manhattan and Brooklyn, while Staten Island stands out for a markedly higher fatality rate relative to its collision count. Inside the vehicle, ejection status and seatbelt use are the strongest predictors of survival — a finding that underscores the protective role of the car body itself. Motorcyclists, who lack that protection entirely, face fatality rates far exceeding any other motorist category. And while driver inattention causes the most crashes, it is speeding and traffic control violations that are most likely to turn a crash fatal — a distinction with direct implications for where enforcement and infrastructure investment should be directed.
